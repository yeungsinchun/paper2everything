Section 2.3

Bohr's model of the hydrogen atom

Bohr kept Rutherford's orbiting electron but allowed only certain orbits. That one rule explains the hydrogen spectrum.

In short

\( E_n = -\dfrac{13.6}{n^2}\ \text{eV} \)energy levels of hydrogen; ground state n = 1

\( hf = E_b - E_a \)one photon per jump, emitted or absorbed

Ionization energy 13.6 eVlifts the electron from n = 1 to n = ∞

Lyman, Balmer, Paschenjumps ending at n = 1, 2, 3: ultraviolet, visible, infrared

Photon: exact match; electron: enoughan electron can give part of its energy

ABohr's four postulates

PostulateWhat it saysClassical or quantum
1. Circular orbitThe electron moves in a circle round the nucleus; the electric attraction provides the centripetal force.Classical (from Rutherford)
2. Stationary orbitsOnly certain orbits are allowed. In them the electron does not radiate, so each orbit has a fixed energy.Quantum
3. TransitionsThe atom emits or absorbs one photon only when the electron jumps between allowed orbits: \(hf = E_{\text{upper}} - E_{\text{lower}}\).Quantum
4. Quantized angular momentumThe allowed orbits are those with \(m_e v r = \dfrac{nh}{2\pi}\), \(n = 1, 2, 3, \ldots\)Quantum
Definition

A quantity is quantized when it can take only certain separate values. The whole number \(n\) that labels an allowed orbit and its energy is the quantum number.

Quick check

Which of these is not one of Bohr's postulates?

B. Bohr required the angular momentum to be quantized, \(nh/2\pi\). A is postulate 2, C postulate 3 and D postulate 1.

BEnergy levels of hydrogen

\[ E_n = -\frac{13.6}{n^{2}}\ \mathrm{eV} \qquad r_n = n^{2} r_1, \quad r_1 = 5.29 \times 10^{-11}\ \mathrm{m} \]

Both formulas apply to hydrogen only. \(r_1\), the radius of the smallest orbit, is the Bohr radius.

TermMeaningHydrogen
Ground stateLowest level, \(n = 1\); the most stable state\(-13.6\) eV
Excited statesEvery level with \(n > 1\); \(n = 2\) is the first excited state\(-3.40, -1.51, -0.85, \ldots\) eV
Excitation energyEnergy to raise the atom from one level to a higher one: \(E_b - E_a\)\(n = 1 \to 2\): 10.2 eV
Ionization energyLeast energy to remove the electron from the ground state: \(E_\infty - E_1 = -E_1\)13.6 eV

Quick check

(a) The gap between neighbouring levels grows as \(n\) increases.

False. The levels crowd together near 0 eV: \(n = 1 \to 2\) needs 10.2 eV but \(n = 3 \to 4\) only 0.66 eV.

(b) A ground-state hydrogen atom can be ionized only by exactly 13.6 eV.

False. 13.6 eV is the minimum. Any extra energy becomes the kinetic energy of the freed electron, since free energies are not quantized.

Quick check

Why are the energy levels of a hydrogen atom negative?

B. A free electron at rest has zero energy; a bound one has less. A confuses charge with energy. C is impossible: kinetic energy is never negative. D contradicts Bohr's stationary orbits.

Worked example: exciting and ionizing hydrogen
Worked example

Find (a) the energy to raise a hydrogen atom from \(n = 2\) to \(n = 3\), and (b) the least energy to ionize an atom in the \(n = 4\) state.

  1. \(E_3 - E_2 = \left(-\dfrac{13.6}{9}\right) - \left(-\dfrac{13.6}{4}\right) = -1.51 + 3.40\).
  2. Ionizing means reaching \(n = \infty\), where \(E = 0\): energy \(= 0 - E_4 = 13.6 / 16\).

(a) 1.89 eV; (b) 0.85 eV

The final state for ionization is \(n = \infty\), not \(n = 1\).

CThe hydrogen spectrum explained

\[ \frac{1}{\lambda} = R\left(\frac{1}{a^{2}} - \frac{1}{b^{2}}\right) \]
\[ R = \frac{13.6\ \mathrm{eV}}{hc} = 1.097 \times 10^{7}\ \mathrm{m^{-1}} \]

\(a\) is the lower level and \(b\) the upper level, \(b > a\). Rydberg found this formula from the measured lines; Bohr's levels explain it, since \(hc/\lambda = E_b - E_a\).

SeriesJumps end atRegionWavelengths
Lyman\(n = 1\)Ultraviolet122 nm down to 91 nm
Balmer\(n = 2\)Visible (and near ultraviolet)656 nm down to 365 nm
Paschen\(n = 3\)Infrared1880 nm down to 820 nm
  • Each series has a longest wavelength (the smallest jump, \(b = a + 1\)) and a shortest one, the series limit (\(b = \infty\)). Lines crowd together towards the limit.
  • Absorption uses the same gaps upwards, so absorption lines sit at exactly the emission wavelengths.
  • An atom in level \(n\) can emit by several routes. From \(n = 4\) there are six possible lines.

Quick check

Hydrogen atoms are all in the \(n = 4\) state. How many different spectral lines can they emit as they fall back to the ground state?

C. Count every pair of levels from 1 to 4: 4→3, 4→2, 4→1, 3→2, 3→1, 2→1. A counts only the jumps from \(n = 4\); D would need five levels.

Quick check

Hydrogen emits a photon when its electron moves from level n = 5 to n = 3. Which spectral band contains the photon?

A. The fourth excited state is \(n = 5\) and the second is \(n = 3\). A jump ending at \(n = 3\) is in the Paschen series: \(1.51 - 0.54 = 0.97\) eV, about 1280 nm. Choosing B usually means reading "second excited" as \(n = 2\).

Worked example: naming a line
Worked example

Hydrogen emits an ultraviolet line of wavelength 97.3 nm in the Lyman series. Which level does the jump start from?

  1. Lyman means \(a = 1\): \(\dfrac{1}{97.3 \times 10^{-9}} = 1.097 \times 10^{7}\left(1 - \dfrac{1}{b^{2}}\right)\).
  2. \(1 - \dfrac{1}{b^{2}} = \dfrac{1.028 \times 10^{7}}{1.097 \times 10^{7}} = 0.937\), so \(\dfrac{1}{b^{2}} = 0.063\).
  3. \(b^{2} = 15.9\).

\(b = 4\): the jump is from \(n = 4\) to \(n = 1\).

DExciting an atom: photons and electrons

IncomingExcites the atom whenAfterwards
PhotonIts energy equals a gap exactly (or exceeds the ionization energy)The photon is absorbed whole and disappears
ElectronIts kinetic energy is at least a gapIt gives up exactly the gap and keeps the rest: an inelastic collision
Either, too little energyNeverPhoton passes through; electron bounces off with no loss of KE: an elastic collision
Exam tip

A photon is all or nothing. An electron can share its energy. Check which one the question uses.

Quick check

Which of these can excite a hydrogen atom in its ground state? (Levels: \(-13.6\), \(-3.40\), \(-1.51\) eV.)

  1. A photon of energy 11.0 eV
  2. An electron of kinetic energy 11.0 eV
  3. A photon of energy 12.09 eV

D. 12.09 eV equals \(E_3 - E_1\) exactly, so that photon is absorbed. The electron needs only at least 10.2 eV. The 11.0 eV photon matches no gap, so (1) fails.

Worked example: an atom with three levels
Worked example

An atom has levels \(-15.0\) eV (ground), \(-7.0\) eV and \(-3.2\) eV. (a) Can it absorb a photon of wavelength 105 nm? (b) An electron of 10.0 eV hits the ground-state atom. What can happen?

  1. Photon energy \(= 1243 / 105 = 11.8\) eV. Gaps from the ground state: \(-7.0 - (-15.0) = 8.0\) eV and \(-3.2 - (-15.0) = 11.8\) eV.
  2. 11.8 eV matches the second gap, so the photon is absorbed and the atom goes to the \(-3.2\) eV level.
  3. 10.0 eV is enough for the 8.0 eV gap but not the 11.8 eV gap.

(a) Yes, to the top level; (b) the atom can rise to \(-7.0\) eV, leaving the electron 2.0 eV of KE (or the collision is elastic and the electron keeps 10.0 eV).

Quick check

A fluorescent paint glows green under ultraviolet light. Why is the emitted light of longer wavelength than the light absorbed?

C. Smaller jumps release smaller photons, so the wavelength is longer. B is backwards. A confuses speed with wavelength. D has the right idea of sharing energy, but the steps are set by the levels and need not be equal.
