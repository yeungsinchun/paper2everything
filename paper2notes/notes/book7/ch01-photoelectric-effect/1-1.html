Photoelectric effect

ALight in, electrons out

Definition

In the photoelectric effect, electromagnetic radiation of high enough frequency falls on a metal surface and electrons are emitted from the surface. The emitted electrons are called photoelectrons; they are ordinary electrons.

A charged zinc plate under ultraviolet light
PlateWhat happensWhy
NegativeLoses its chargeUltraviolet frees electrons; the negative plate repels them, so they leave for good.
PositiveKeeps its chargeElectrons are still freed, but the positive plate attracts them straight back.
ConclusionThe light ejects electrons from the metal. If it only ionized the air, both plates would lose charge.

Quick check

An insulated zinc plate carries a positive charge. Ultraviolet light shines on it. What happens to the charge on the plate?

B. Electrons are freed, but the positive plate pulls them back, so the charge hardly changes. A is what a negative plate does. C would need the plate to gain electrons. D fails because any electron that escapes makes the plate more positive and pulls the next one back harder.

Quick check

(a) Photoelectrons are a different kind of particle from electrons.

False. "Photo" only says how they were freed. They are ordinary electrons.

(b) X-rays falling on a metal can also cause the photoelectric effect.

True. X-rays have a higher frequency than ultraviolet, so they free electrons even more easily.

BThe photocell and its current

Definition

A photocell is an evacuated tube holding a metal cathode, which emits photoelectrons, and an anode, which collects them. The photocurrent is proportional to the number of photoelectrons reaching the anode each second.

\[ I = n e \]

\(I\) = photocurrent (A), \(n\) = photoelectrons reaching the anode per second, \(e = 1.60 \times 10^{-19}\ \mathrm{C}\).

Anode voltageCurrentReason
Positive, largeConstant: the saturation currentEvery photoelectron is pulled to the anode.
Negative, smallSmallerThe anode repels electrons; only the faster ones arrive.
\(-V_s\) or belowZeroNot even the fastest electron arrives.
Exam tip

Brighter light of the same frequency raises only the saturation current. The stopping potential stays the same.

Quick check

Light of fixed frequency falls on a photocell. The intensity is doubled. Which quantities increase?

  1. The saturation photocurrent
  2. The stopping potential
  3. The maximum kinetic energy of the photoelectrons

A. More light per second frees more electrons per second, so only the current rises. The fastest electron is no faster, so (2) and (3) stay the same. C and D are the wave-theory guess that brighter light gives faster electrons.

Quick check

The saturation current of a photocell is 0.32 μA. How many photoelectrons leave the cathode each second?

C. \(n = I/e = 0.32 \times 10^{-6} / 1.60 \times 10^{-19} = 2.0 \times 10^{12}\) per second. At saturation every emitted electron arrives. A multiplies instead of dividing; B drops the "micro"; D forgets to divide by 1.60.

CStopping potential measures the fastest electron

Definition

The stopping potential \(V_s\) is the smallest reverse voltage that stops every photoelectron from reaching the anode. The cathode still emits electrons; they just cannot arrive.

\[ eV_s = K_{\max} = \tfrac{1}{2} m v_{\max}^{2} \]

\(V_s\) in V, \(K_{\max}\) in J, \(m = 9.11 \times 10^{-31}\ \mathrm{kg}\). In electronvolts the numbers match: a stopping potential of 2.1 V means \(K_{\max} = 2.1\ \mathrm{eV}\).

Exam tip

With an accelerating (positive) anode voltage \(V\), the fastest electron arrives with \(K_{\max} + eV\).

Quick check

Photoelectrons leave a cathode with maximum kinetic energy 1.2 eV. The anode is at +2.0 V. What is the greatest kinetic energy of an electron reaching the anode?

D. An attracting anode adds \(eV = 2.0\) eV to the 1.2 eV the electron leaves with. A treats the anode as repelling. B ignores the field. C forgets the starting energy.

Quick check

(a) At the stopping potential, no photoelectrons are emitted from the cathode.

False. Emission depends on the light, not the anode. The electrons are emitted but turned back before the anode.

Worked example
Worked example

The stopping potential in a photocell is 2.10 V. Find the maximum kinetic energy of the photoelectrons in eV and in J, and their maximum speed.

  1. \(K_{\max} = eV_s\), so \(K_{\max} = 2.10\ \mathrm{eV}\).
  2. In joules: \(2.10 \times 1.60 \times 10^{-19} = 3.36 \times 10^{-19}\ \mathrm{J}\).
  3. \(v_{\max} = \sqrt{2K_{\max}/m} = \sqrt{2 \times 3.36 \times 10^{-19} / 9.11 \times 10^{-31}}\).

\(v_{\max} = 8.59 \times 10^{5}\ \mathrm{m\,s^{-1}}\)

DWhere the wave theory fails

Definition

The threshold frequency \(f_0\) of a metal is the lowest frequency of light that can emit photoelectrons from it. Below \(f_0\) nothing is emitted, however intense the light.

TestWave theory predictsExperiment shows
Which light worksAny frequency, if bright enough or given enough timeOnly \(f > f_0\); below it, no emission at any intensity
Fastest electronFaster in brighter light; frequency does not matter\(K_{\max}\) does not depend on intensity; it rises with frequency
DelayDim light needs time to build up enough energyEmission starts at once, even in dim light
Number emittedMore in brighter lightMore in brighter light (the only agreement)

Quick check

Which observation of the photoelectric effect can the wave theory of light explain?

D. More wave energy per second can free more electrons. Waves predict the opposite of A, B and C: any frequency should work, brighter light should give faster electrons, and dim light should need time.

Worked example: the wave-theory delay
Worked example

Light of intensity \(0.05\ \mathrm{W\,m^{-2}}\) falls on sodium. Suppose, as the wave theory says, that one atom collects energy over an area of \(1 \times 10^{-20}\ \mathrm{m^2}\). An electron needs 2.3 eV to escape. How long must it wait?

  1. Power reaching the atom \(= 0.05 \times 1 \times 10^{-20} = 5 \times 10^{-22}\ \mathrm{W}\).
  2. Energy needed \(= 2.3 \times 1.60 \times 10^{-19} = 3.68 \times 10^{-19}\ \mathrm{J}\).
  3. Time \(= 3.68 \times 10^{-19} / 5 \times 10^{-22}\).

About 740 s, over 12 minutes. Experiments find no measurable delay, so the wave picture fails.
