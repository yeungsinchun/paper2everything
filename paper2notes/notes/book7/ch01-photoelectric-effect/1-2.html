ALight comes in photons

Definition

A photon is a packet (quantum) of electromagnetic energy. Its energy depends only on the frequency of the light: higher frequency, more energy per photon.

\[ E = hf = \frac{hc}{\lambda} \]

\(h = 6.63 \times 10^{-34}\ \mathrm{J\,s}\) (Planck constant), \(c = 3.00 \times 10^{8}\ \mathrm{m\,s^{-1}}\). Convert with \(1\ \mathrm{eV} = 1.60 \times 10^{-19}\ \mathrm{J}\). A handy shortcut: \(E\) in eV \(= 1243 / \lambda\) in nm.

LightWavelengthFrequencyPhoton energy
Red700 nm\(4.3 \times 10^{14}\) Hz1.8 eV
Green530 nm\(5.7 \times 10^{14}\) Hz2.3 eV
Violet400 nm\(7.5 \times 10^{14}\) Hz3.1 eV
Ultraviolet250 nm\(1.2 \times 10^{15}\) Hz5.0 eV
\[ P = N\,hf \]

A beam of power \(P\) delivers \(N\) photons per second. Intensity is power per unit area.

Quick check

A red beam and a blue beam have the same power. Which statement is correct?

A. Same power, \(P = Nhf\): red photons have lower \(f\), so more of them are needed. B reverses this. C forgets that photon energy depends on frequency. D is backwards: blue has the higher frequency.

Worked example: photons from a laser pointer
Worked example

A laser pointer emits 1.0 mW of light of wavelength 650 nm. How many photons does it emit per second?

  1. Photon energy \(E = hc/\lambda = (6.63 \times 10^{-34})(3.00 \times 10^{8}) / (650 \times 10^{-9}) = 3.06 \times 10^{-19}\ \mathrm{J}\) (1.91 eV).
  2. \(N = P/E = 1.0 \times 10^{-3} / 3.06 \times 10^{-19}\).

\(N = 3.3 \times 10^{15}\) photons per second

BEinstein's photoelectric equation

Definition

The work function \(\varphi\) of a metal is the minimum energy needed to remove an electron from its surface.

\[ K_{\max} = hf - \varphi \qquad \varphi = hf_0 \qquad \lambda_0 = \frac{hc}{\varphi} \]

If \(hf < \varphi\) (\(f < f_0\), or \(\lambda > \lambda_0\)), no electron escapes. \(\lambda_0\) is the threshold wavelength: the longest wavelength that still emits.

Quick check

(a) One photon can free at most one electron.

True. A photon is absorbed whole by a single electron and then no longer exists.

(b) Every photoelectron leaves the metal with kinetic energy \(hf - \varphi\).

False. \(\varphi\) is the minimum escape energy. Electrons from deeper inside need more, so most leave with less than \(K_{\max}\).

Quick check

Photons of energy 6.0 eV fall on a metal of work function 4.0 eV. What is the stopping potential?

A. \(K_{\max} = 6.0 - 4.0 = 2.0\) eV, so \(V_s = 2.0\) V. B is the work function, C the photon energy, D adds them.

Worked example: sodium in violet light
Worked example

Sodium has work function 2.3 eV. Find its threshold frequency and wavelength. Violet light of wavelength 400 nm then falls on it: find \(K_{\max}\) and the fastest electron's speed.

  1. \(f_0 = \varphi / h = (2.3 \times 1.60 \times 10^{-19}) / 6.63 \times 10^{-34} = 5.55 \times 10^{14}\ \mathrm{Hz}\).
  2. \(\lambda_0 = c / f_0 = 3.00 \times 10^{8} / 5.55 \times 10^{14} = 5.4 \times 10^{-7}\ \mathrm{m}\) (540 nm).
  3. Photon energy \(= 1243 / 400 = 3.11\ \mathrm{eV}\). \(K_{\max} = 3.11 - 2.3 = 0.81\ \mathrm{eV} = 1.29 \times 10^{-19}\ \mathrm{J}\).
  4. \(v_{\max} = \sqrt{2 \times 1.29 \times 10^{-19} / 9.11 \times 10^{-31}}\).

\(f_0 = 5.55 \times 10^{14}\) Hz, \(\lambda_0 = 540\) nm, \(K_{\max} = 0.81\) eV, \(v_{\max} = 5.3 \times 10^{5}\ \mathrm{m\,s^{-1}}\)

Keep units consistent: subtract eV from eV or J from J, never a mix.

CPhotons explain all four features

FeaturePhoton explanation
Threshold frequencyBelow \(f_0\), one photon has less energy than \(\varphi\). Electrons cannot add up several photons, so none escape.
\(K_{\max}\) rises with \(f\)\(K_{\max} = hf - \varphi\): a higher frequency means a bigger photon and more energy left over.
\(K_{\max}\) ignores intensityBrighter light means more photons, not bigger ones.
No delayOne photon delivers its whole energy at once; nothing needs to build up.
Rate \(\propto\) intensityMore photons per second free more electrons per second.

Quick check

\(K_{\max}\) is plotted against frequency for two different metals. Which feature is the same for both lines?

C. The slope is \(h\), a universal constant. The intercepts \(f_0\) and \(-\varphi\) belong to each metal, so A and B differ.

DMeasuring \(h\), reading I–V curves

\[ V_s = \frac{h}{e}\, f - \frac{\varphi}{e} \]
Exam tip

Light behaves as a wave when it interferes and as particles when it is absorbed or emitted. Both pictures are needed.

Quick check

In Fig. 1.9, curve R was taken after a single change to the light used for curve P. What was the change?

B. R reaches zero at a more negative voltage, so its stopping potential, and hence its frequency, is larger. Its saturation current is unchanged, so the photon rate is the same. A and D would change only the saturation level.

Worked example: h and φ from two readings
Worked example

Light of \(6.0 \times 10^{14}\) Hz gives a stopping potential of 0.20 V; light of \(8.0 \times 10^{14}\) Hz gives 1.03 V. Find \(h\) and the work function.

  1. Slope \(= (1.03 - 0.20) / (8.0 - 6.0) \times 10^{14} = 4.15 \times 10^{-15}\ \mathrm{V\,s}\).
  2. \(h = e \times \text{slope} = 1.60 \times 10^{-19} \times 4.15 \times 10^{-15} = 6.6 \times 10^{-34}\ \mathrm{J\,s}\).
  3. \(\varphi / e = \text{slope} \times f - V_s = 4.15 \times 10^{-15} \times 6.0 \times 10^{14} - 0.20 = 2.29\ \mathrm{V}\).

\(h \approx 6.6 \times 10^{-34}\ \mathrm{J\,s}\), \(\varphi \approx 2.3\ \mathrm{eV}\)

Worked example: not every photon frees an electron
Worked example

2.0 mW of 400 nm light falls on a cathode and the saturation current is 1.6 μA. What fraction of the photons free an electron?

  1. Photons per second \(= P / (hc/\lambda) = 2.0 \times 10^{-3} / 4.97 \times 10^{-19} = 4.0 \times 10^{15}\).
  2. Electrons per second \(= I/e = 1.6 \times 10^{-6} / 1.60 \times 10^{-19} = 1.0 \times 10^{13}\).

\(1.0 \times 10^{13} / 4.0 \times 10^{15} = 0.25\%\): about 1 photon in 400. The rest are reflected or absorbed without freeing an electron.
