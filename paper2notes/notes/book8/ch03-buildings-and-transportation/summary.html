Goals

After this chapter you can:

  • use the law of conduction and U-values to find heat flow through a layer

  • find the OTTV of a building and explain what raises or lowers it

  • explain how insulation, double glazing, low-e coatings, shading and orientation cut heat gain

  • compare the energy conversions in petrol, electric and hybrid vehicles, including regenerative braking

  • explain why mass transport saves energy, and use \( P = Fv \) for a car

2Definitions

Building envelope
The roofs, walls and windows that separate a building from outside
Thermal conductivity \( \kappa \)
How well a material conducts heat; W m⁻¹ K⁻¹; set by the material alone
U-value
\( \kappa/d \) of one layer; W m⁻² K⁻¹; small means good insulation
OTTV
Average heat gain through the envelope per unit area over a year; W m⁻²
Regenerative braking
The motor works as a generator while braking and recharges the battery
Hybrid electric vehicle
A vehicle with an engine and a battery-powered motor, chosen by a computer

4Traps that cost marks

κ or U?

\( \kappa \) is the material's; \( U = \kappa/d \) is the layer's. Only \( U \) changes with thickness.

U is not P

U does not depend on area or temperature difference; the heat flow \( P = UA\Delta T \) does.

OTTV is the envelope only

People, lamps and appliances add air-conditioning load but not OTTV. Open-window convection is not counted.

Batteries store chemical energy

Regenerative braking: KE → electrical → chemical.

Hybrids still burn fuel

An HEV's energy all comes from fuel; it just wastes less. It is not emission-free.

Power is not ∝ v³ exactly

Rolling friction stays constant, so \( P = (f_r + k_a v^2)v \) rises a little less steeply than \( v^3 \).
