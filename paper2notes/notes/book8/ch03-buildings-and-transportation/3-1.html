Section 3.1

Energy efficiency in buildings

How heat gets into a building, how to measure it, and how designers keep it out.

AHow heat gets into a building

Definition

The building envelope is the outer shell of a building: its roofs, walls and windows. All heat entering or leaving passes through it.

  • Conduction: a sun-baked roof or wall passes heat inwards, so top-floor flats run hotter.
  • Radiation: sunlight through glass heats a room directly; a large west-facing window makes a room hot in the late afternoon.

Quick check

True or false?

A top-floor flat gains extra heat by conduction through the sunlit roof.

The roof is heated directly by the sun, so a large temperature difference drives heat down through it.

Glass lets heat into a building by radiation as well as by conduction.

Glass conducts about as well as concrete, but it also lets sunlight and infrared straight through. Concrete blocks radiation.

BThe law of conduction

Definition

Thermal conductivity \( \kappa \) measures how well a material conducts heat. Its unit is W m⁻¹ K⁻¹; it depends only on the material.

\[ P = \frac{Q}{t} = \frac{\kappa A \,\Delta T}{d} \]
  • \( P \): rate of heat flow (W); \( A \): area of the layer (m²); \( \Delta T = T_\text{hot} - T_\text{cold} \) across it (K or °C); \( d \): thickness (m).
  • Metals conduct well (large \( \kappa \)); non-metals and trapped gases conduct poorly.

Quick check

A layer keeps the same temperature difference across it. Its area is doubled and its thickness halved. The rate of heat flow becomes

\( P \propto A/d \): doubling \( A \) doubles it and halving \( d \) doubles it again, so ×4. A assumes the two changes cancel.

CU-value: how well a layer insulates

Definition

The U-value (thermal transmittance) of a layer is \( \kappa/d \). It says how readily that particular layer conducts heat; its unit is W m⁻² K⁻¹.

\[ \begin{gathered} U = \frac{\kappa}{d} \\ P = UA\,\Delta T \end{gathered} \]
  • Good insulation means a small \( U \): a thick layer of a low-conductivity material.
  • \( U \) depends on the material and the thickness only, not on the area or the temperature difference.
Common mistake \( \kappa \) belongs to a material; \( U \) belongs to a layer. Two concrete walls have the same \( \kappa \) but different \( U \) if their thicknesses differ.

Quick check

Which of these affect the U-value of a wall?

  1. its area
  2. its thickness
  3. the material it is made of

\( U = \kappa/d \): the material sets \( \kappa \) and the thickness is \( d \). Area changes the total heat flow \( P = UA\Delta T \), but not \( U \).

Worked example

A brick wall of area 12 m² is 0.20 m thick, with \( \kappa = 0.70 \) W m⁻¹ K⁻¹. Its inner surface is at 22 °C and its outer surface at 6 °C. Find its U-value and the rate of heat flow. Which way does the heat go?

Show solution
  1. \( U = 0.70 / 0.20 = 3.5\ \text{W m}^{-2}\,\text{K}^{-1} \).
  2. \( P = UA\Delta T = 3.5 \times 12 \times 16 = 672\ \text{W} \).
  3. Heat flows from hot to cold: out of the building.

U = 3.5 W m⁻² K⁻¹; about 670 W lost through the wall.

DOverall Thermal Transfer Value (OTTV)

Definition

The Overall Thermal Transfer Value (OTTV) describes yearly heat entering through a building envelope, divided by the envelope area. Measure it in W m⁻².

\[ \text{OTTV} = \frac{P_\text{total}}{A_\text{total}} = \frac{P_1 + P_2 + \cdots + P_n}{A_1 + A_2 + \cdots + A_n} \]
  • It counts heat gained through the envelope only, by conduction and by radiation through windows. It ignores heat from people, lamps and appliances, and from the ground, and it assumes the windows are shut.
  • A lower OTTV means heat enters more slowly, so less air-conditioning is needed. Hong Kong sets upper limits on OTTV for new commercial buildings.

Quick check

Does each change lower the OTTV of an office building?

Replacing every filament lamp inside with an LED lamp lowers the OTTV.

Heat from lamps is inside the building, not through the envelope, so the OTTV is unchanged. It does cut the air-conditioning load.

Replacing part of a sunlit concrete wall with clear glass lowers the OTTV.

Glass lets in solar radiation that concrete blocks, so the heat gain, and the OTTV, go up.

Worked example

A site hut has walls and roof of total area 60 m² with \( U = 2.0 \) W m⁻² K⁻¹, and their outer surfaces are 5 °C warmer than the inner ones. Its windows, of total area 6 m², gain 900 W by radiation (ignore conduction through them). Find the OTTV.

Show solution
  1. Conduction through walls and roof: \( UA\Delta T = 2.0 \times 60 \times 5 = 600\ \text{W} \).
  2. Total gain \( = 600 + 900 = 1500\ \text{W} \); total envelope area \( = 60 + 6 = 66\ \text{m}^2 \).
  3. \( \text{OTTV} = 1500 / 66 \approx 22.7\ \text{W m}^{-2} \).

About 23 W m⁻². The small windows supply most of the gain.

Quick check

A film for office windows should cut air-conditioning without darkening the rooms. Which transmission does it need?

Infrared carries heat in, so block it; visible light gives daylight, so let it through. C would keep heat out but also force the lights on.
