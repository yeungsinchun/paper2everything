Summary: Cooking and Air-Conditioning

Formulas, definitions, the cookers side by side, and the traps.

Goals

After this chapter you can:

  • explain how a hotplate, an induction cooker and a microwave oven heat food, and where each loses energy

  • measure and compare the end-use efficiency of cookers using \( mc\Delta T \)

  • explain how a heat pump moves heat from cold to hot, using \( Q_H = Q_C + W \)

  • use cooling capacity and COP, and describe the refrigeration cycle

  • use the energy label of an air conditioner to find its input power, COP and running cost

2Definitions

Eddy current
A current induced in a solid conductor by a changing magnetic field
Polar molecule
A molecule with a slightly positive end and a slightly negative end, such as water
Heat pump
A device that uses work to move heat from a colder region to a hotter one
Cooling capacity
Heat removed per unit time by an air conditioner (W)
COP
Heat removed divided by the work done to remove it; no unit
Refrigerant
The fluid that absorbs heat as it evaporates and releases it as it condenses

4Traps that cost marks

Where the heat is made

Hotplate: in the element, then conducted to the pot. Induction: in the pot itself; the glass top only gets warm from the pot.

COP above 1 is normal

COP is not an efficiency. Moving 3 J of heat with 1 J of work gives COP 3.

QH is the biggest

Heat dumped outdoors is the room's heat plus the work: \( Q_H = Q_C + W \).

Evaporate in, condense out

The refrigerant absorbs latent heat as it evaporates indoors and releases it as it condenses outdoors.

Power from a label

Average input power = annual consumption ÷ 1200 h; then COP = capacity ÷ power.

Microwaves are not eddy currents

Microwave ovens act on polar water molecules; induction cookers act on conducting pots.
