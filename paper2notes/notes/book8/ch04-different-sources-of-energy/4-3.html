Section 4.3

Energy, society and environment

What using energy does to the air and the climate, how energy can be stored, and how Hong Kong tracks its use.

AAir pollution from energy use

  • Electricity looks clean where it is used. Pollution happens elsewhere in its life: extracting, refining and transporting fuel; building, running and maintaining power stations; and taking them down.
  • The main pollutants are sulphur dioxide (SO₂), nitrogen oxides (NOₓ), volatile organic compounds, particulates and greenhouse gases.
PollutantHarm it can do
Sulphur dioxideAcid rain; breathing problems
Nitrogen oxidesAcid rain; photochemical smog; breathing problems; too much algae and weed growth in rivers
Volatile organic compoundsSmog; some, such as benzene, cause cancer
Carbon monoxideCuts the oxygen the blood carries to the organs
ParticulatesIrritate the eyes and lungs
  • Cutting emissions: scrub SO₂ and NOₓ from power-station flue gas; burn low-sulphur coal or natural gas; use cleaner diesel; fit better catalytic converters.
  • Other waste: nuclear plants make radioactive waste; every thermal power station dumps waste heat into its cooling water. Spent nuclear fuel can be reprocessed to recycle fuel and shrink the high-level waste. Waste heat can be recovered, for example to make hot water.

Quick check

Put these in descending order of pollution per kW h of electricity: (1) coal, (2) natural gas, (3) solar cells.

Coal is the dirtiest fuel, gas burns cleaner, and solar cells pollute only when they are made and installed.

Quick check

True or false?

Renewable energy sources do no harm to the environment at all.

Building turbines, dams and solar cells causes emissions, and dams and wind farms affect wildlife.

A nuclear power station releases sulphur dioxide during normal operation.

Nothing is burnt. In normal running it releases warm cooling water and water vapour; its radioactive waste is kept and stored, not released.

BStoring energy

  • Solar and wind output rise and fall with the weather, and demand changes through the day. Spare energy must be stored, or it is wasted.
  • Pumped storage: when demand is low, spare electricity pumps water up to a high reservoir (electrical → gravitational PE). When demand is high, the water runs back down through turbines like a hydroelectric station.
  • Compressed air: spare electricity pumps air into a sealed cave; later the air is released to drive a turbine. Batteries and heat stores are other options.

Worked example

A pumped-storage system moves 1.0 × 10⁶ m³ of water to a reservoir 500 m above the turbines. With water density 1000 kg m⁻³, g = 9.81 m s⁻² and an overall cycle efficiency of 75%, determine the electrical energy recovered, in kW h.

Show solution
  1. Mass: \( 1.0 \times 10^{6} \times 1000 = 1.0 \times 10^{9}\ \text{kg} \).
  2. PE stored: \( mgh = (1.0 \times 10^{9})(9.81)(500) = 4.9 \times 10^{12}\ \text{J} \).
  3. Energy back: \( 0.75 \times 4.9 \times 10^{12} = 3.7 \times 10^{12}\ \text{J} = \dfrac{3.7 \times 10^{12}}{3.6 \times 10^{6}} = 1.0 \times 10^{6}\ \text{kW h} \).

About a million kW h. A quarter of the stored energy is lost, but nothing generated at night is thrown away.

CThe greenhouse effect

  • The Earth absorbs about 10¹⁷ J of solar energy every second and radiates the same amount back to space, so its temperature stays steady.
  • With no atmosphere, the average surface temperature would be about −18 °C. It is actually about 15 °C. The 33 °C difference is the greenhouse effect.
  • Sunlight is mostly short wavelength and passes through the atmosphere. The warmed ground gives off long-wavelength infrared.
  • Greenhouse gases absorb that infrared and re-emit it in all directions, some of it back down. The ground is heated by the Sun and by the atmosphere, so more energy is trapped.
  • Water vapour gives most of the natural effect. Carbon dioxide, methane, nitrous oxide, ozone and fluorinated gases add to it. Oxygen and nitrogen are not greenhouse gases.
Definition

The enhanced greenhouse effect is the extra warming caused by greenhouse gases that human activity has added since the 1750s: burning fuels, industry, transport and livestock.

  • Apart from water vapour, CO₂ adds the most to global warming; global temperature has risen as its concentration has risen.
  • A warmer atmosphere holds more water vapour, which strengthens the effect further.
  • Likely results: a warmer Earth on average with more extreme hot and cold spells; more evaporation and rain, with some regions wetter and others drier; rising sea level as ice melts and seawater expands; changed habitats.
Common mistake The greenhouse effect itself is natural and keeps the Earth habitable. The problem is the enhanced effect from extra gases.

Worked example

The solar constant is 1360 W m⁻² and the Earth's radius is 6.4 × 10⁶ m. The Earth reflects 30% of the sunlight reaching it. Estimate the solar power the Earth absorbs.

Show solution
  1. Seen from the Sun, the Earth is a disc of area \( \pi R^2 = \pi (6.4 \times 10^{6})^2 = 1.29 \times 10^{14}\ \text{m}^2 \).
  2. Power absorbed \( = 0.70 \times 1360 \times 1.29 \times 10^{14} = 1.2 \times 10^{17}\ \text{W} \).

About 1.2 × 10¹⁷ W. The intercepting area is a disc, \( \pi R^2 \), not the sphere's surface.

Quick check

Is each of these a greenhouse gas?

Oxygen is a greenhouse gas.

Oxygen and nitrogen hardly absorb infrared, even though they make up most of the air.

Methane is a greenhouse gas.

Methane absorbs and re-emits infrared strongly. Livestock and landfills release it.

Carbon dioxide from burning fossil fuels is the only greenhouse gas that people release.

People also release methane, nitrous oxide and fluorinated gases.

DEnergy use in Hong Kong

  • The government publishes Hong Kong Energy End-use Data every year. It shows which fuels are used and how much energy each sector uses: residential, commercial, industrial and transport.
  • Space conditioning (heating and cooling buildings) is the largest single end use. The commercial sector uses the most electricity.
  • Total energy use keeps rising, driven by a growing population and by keeping tall buildings comfortable.
  • To use less: choose efficient appliances (Chapters 1–2), make buildings and vehicles efficient (Chapter 3), move to cleaner sources, and cut waste. Waste can itself be a source: landfill gas and sewage biogas already generate electricity, and incinerators can too.
Common mistake When reading end-use charts, a percentage share is not an amount. A sector's share can fall while its actual energy use rises, if the total grows.

Worked example

Each day, a tall building sends 200 m³ of water to a rooftop tank 120 m above the pumps. Find the ideal daily energy input in kW h. Take water density as 1000 kg m⁻³ and g = 9.81 m s⁻².

Show solution
  1. Mass: \( 200 \times 1000 = 2.0 \times 10^{5}\ \text{kg} \).
  2. \( mgh = (2.0 \times 10^{5})(9.81)(120) = 2.35 \times 10^{8}\ \text{J} \).
  3. \( 2.35 \times 10^{8} / 3.6 \times 10^{6} = 65\ \text{kW h} \).

At least 65 kW h a day; a real pump needs more. Tall buildings cost energy in ways a low house does not.
