Section 4.2

Energy sources

Fossil fuels, nuclear, wind, water and sunlight: how each makes electricity, how much, and at what cost.

AFossil fuels

  • Coal, oil and natural gas formed from plants and animals buried for around 100 million years. They are burnt to boil water for power stations and to run vehicles.
  • They still supply most of the world's energy, and world energy use keeps rising.
  • They cannot be replaced on any human timescale, so other sources matter more and more.

Quick check

True or false?

Natural gas is renewable because new gas is always forming underground.

It forms over many millions of years, far slower than we burn it, so it counts as non-renewable.

BBinding energy and mass defect

  • Protons repel each other, yet a nucleus holds together. A strong, short-range attractive force binds all the nucleons (protons and neutrons).
  • Pulling a nucleus apart into separate nucleons takes work. That work is its binding energy \( E_b \).
Definition

The binding energy \( E_b \) of a nucleus is the energy needed to separate it into its individual protons and neutrons. The average binding energy per nucleon is \( E_b/A \), where \( A \) is the mass number.

  • The higher \( E_b/A \), the more stable the nucleus. \( E_b/A \) rises from hydrogen to a peak at iron, then falls gently.
  • Energy is released whenever the products sit higher on the curve: fusion of light nuclei, or fission of a heavy nucleus.
  • The products have less mass than the reactants. The lost mass \( \Delta m \) is the mass defect, and it appears as energy.
\[ \begin{gathered} \Delta E = \Delta m\,c^2 \\ 1\ \text{u} \equiv 931\ \text{MeV} \\ 1\ \text{eV} = 1.60 \times 10^{-19}\ \text{J} \end{gathered} \]
Common mistake Less stable does not mean it reacts more easily. Hydrogen nuclei are less stable than uranium, but fusing them needs a huge temperature to push them together.

Worked example

A deuteron (hydrogen-2 nucleus) has mass 2.01355 u. A proton has mass 1.00728 u and a neutron 1.00866 u. Find the binding energy of the deuteron in MeV, and its binding energy per nucleon.

Show solution
  1. Mass of the separate nucleons: \( 1.00728 + 1.00866 = 2.01594\ \text{u} \).
  2. Mass defect: \( \Delta m = 2.01594 - 2.01355 = 0.00239\ \text{u} \).
  3. \( E_b = 0.00239 \times 931 = 2.23\ \text{MeV} \); with \( A = 2 \), \( E_b/A = 1.11\ \text{MeV} \).

About 2.2 MeV, or 1.1 MeV per nucleon: the low left end of Fig. 4.2.

Quick check

Which nucleus is the most stable?

Iron-56 sits at the peak of the curve: it has the largest binding energy per nucleon. D confuses a large total binding energy with a large value per nucleon.

CFission and the chain reaction

  • A uranium-235 nucleus captures a slow neutron, becomes unstable and splits into two smaller nuclei. It also releases 2 or 3 fast neutrons and about 200 MeV.
  • Those neutrons can split more U-235 nuclei: a chain reaction.
  • Natural uranium is only about 0.7% U-235, so reactor fuel is enriched to about 3–4% U-235.

Worked example

One possible fission is \( ^{235}_{92}\text{U} + {}^{1}_{0}\text{n} \rightarrow {}^{141}_{56}\text{Ba} + {}^{92}_{36}\text{Kr} + 3\,{}^{1}_{0}\text{n} \). The masses are U-235: 235.0439 u, Ba-141: 140.9144 u, Kr-92: 91.9262 u, neutron: 1.0087 u, with 1 u ≡ 931 MeV and 1 MeV = 1.60 × 10⁻¹³ J. (a) Find the energy released by one fission. (b) A reactor produces heat at 3000 MW. How many fissions happen each second?

Show solution
  1. Mass before: \( 235.0439 + 1.0087 = 236.0526\ \text{u} \). Mass after: \( 140.9144 + 91.9262 + 3(1.0087) = 235.8667\ \text{u} \).
  2. \( \Delta m = 0.1859\ \text{u} \), so \( \Delta E = 0.1859 \times 931 = 173\ \text{MeV} = 2.77 \times 10^{-11}\ \text{J} \).
  3. Fissions per second \( = \dfrac{3000 \times 10^{6}}{2.77 \times 10^{-11}} = 1.08 \times 10^{20} \).

(a) About 173 MeV per fission. (b) About 1.1 × 10²⁰ fissions each second.

DInside a nuclear power station

PartJob
Fuel rodsHold the enriched uranium (as uranium dioxide) where fission happens.
Control rodsAbsorb spare neutrons. Pushing them deeper slows the chain reaction, so they set the power output.
ModeratorSlows fast neutrons so that U-235 can capture them. Without it the chain reaction dies out.
Pressurized water (loop 1)Acts as the moderator and carries heat from the fuel rods to the boiler. It stays liquid at about 300 °C and is radioactive.
Boiler, turbine, generator (loop 2)Steam turns the turbine, which drives the generator. A condenser turns the steam back into water.
Seawater (loop 3)Cools the condenser and carries the waste heat away. It never touches the fuel.
Common mistake A moderator speeds up the chain reaction by slowing neutrons down. A control rod slows the chain reaction by absorbing neutrons. Do not swap them.
  • Safety: the reactor can be shut down in more than one way (drop all control rods, or inject a neutron absorber). Independent sensors monitor it. A thick reinforced building isolates it. Radiation outside is monitored.
  • Waste: spent fuel stays on site until its activity falls, then is sealed and buried deep underground.
  • Fusion reactors are still experimental: so far they need about as much energy in as they give out.
AdvantagesDrawbacks
No carbon dioxide or smoke while running; small volume of wasteWaste is radioactive with long half-lives, so disposal is costly
Huge energy from a small mass of fuelClosing down a plant is slow and expensive
Output is steady, controllable and weather-independentAn accident can spread radiation over a wide area

Quick check

True or false?

The moderator is used to stop the chain reaction.

The moderator keeps the chain reaction going by slowing neutrons. Control rods are what slow or stop it.

Control rods must be made of a material that absorbs neutrons without splitting.

If a control rod split when hit, it would release more neutrons and feed the chain reaction instead of slowing it.

Energy is released in fission but must be supplied in fusion.

Both release energy, because both move nuclei up the binding energy curve. Fusion needs a high temperature to start, but it gives out far more than that.

EWind power

  • The Sun heats the Earth unevenly, which sets up pressure differences that drive the wind. Wind power is solar energy at second hand.
  • Wind turns the blades and rotor; a gearbox raises the speed for the generator.
  • In time \( t \), a cylinder of air of volume \( Avt \) reaches the blades. Its mass is \( m = \rho A v t \), so the mass per second is \( \rho A v \).
  • Its kinetic energy per second is \( \tfrac12 (\rho A v) v^2 \). That is the most power the wind could give.
\[ \begin{gathered} P_\text{max} = \tfrac12 \rho A v^3 \\ P = \eta P_\text{max} = \tfrac12 \eta \rho A v^3 \\ A = \pi r^2 \end{gathered} \]
  • The air must keep moving after the blades, so \( \eta \) can never pass about 0.6. Friction and electrical resistance lower it further; good turbines reach about 0.45–0.5.
  • \( P \propto v^3 \) and \( P \propto r^2 \): a windy site and long blades matter most.
Common mistake \( A \) is the area of the circle swept by the blades, with the blade length as the radius. It is not the area of the blades themselves.

Worked example

A turbine has blades 30 m long. Wind of speed 8 m s⁻¹ blows straight at them. Air has density 1.2 kg m⁻³ and the turbine efficiency is 0.40. Find the electrical power output.

Show solution
  1. \( A = \pi r^2 = \pi (30)^2 = 2830\ \text{m}^2 \).
  2. \( P_\text{max} = \tfrac12 (1.2)(2830)(8)^3 = 8.69 \times 10^{5}\ \text{W} \).
  3. \( P = 0.40 \times 8.69 \times 10^{5} = 3.47 \times 10^{5}\ \text{W} \).

About 350 kW.

AdvantagesDrawbacks
No harmful by-products while runningA wind farm needs a lot of land or sea

Quick check

The wind speed at a turbine rises from 5 m s⁻¹ to 10 m s⁻¹. The efficiency stays the same. Its power output becomes

\( P \propto v^3 \), and \( 2^3 = 8 \). B forgets that the mass of air per second also doubles.

FHydroelectric power

  • A dam holds a reservoir high up. Water falls through pipes to turbines below, so its gravitational PE becomes KE and then electrical energy.
  • The Sun evaporates the water that falls as rain on high ground, so this is also indirect solar energy.
\[ \begin{gathered} P = \eta \times \frac{m}{t}\,g h \\ \frac{m}{t} = \rho \times (\text{volume flow rate}) \end{gathered} \]
  • Use the difference between the water levels on the two sides of the dam as \( h \).

Worked example

A hydroelectric plant receives 150 m³ of water each second. The reservoir surface is 80 m above the turbine, and the generator system converts 80% of the falling water's lost gravitational energy. Use water density 1000 kg m⁻³ and g = 9.81 m s⁻² to calculate its electrical power.

Show solution
  1. Mass per second: \( 150 \times 1000 = 1.5 \times 10^{5}\ \text{kg s}^{-1} \).
  2. PE lost per second: \( (1.5 \times 10^{5})(9.81)(80) = 1.18 \times 10^{8}\ \text{W} \).
  3. Output: \( 0.80 \times 1.18 \times 10^{8} = 9.4 \times 10^{7}\ \text{W} \).

About 94 MW. Do not forget the efficiency.

AdvantagesDrawbacks
No harmful by-products while runningChanges river flow and harms fish and other river life
Cheap to run (though the dam is costly to build)Flooding the reservoir drowns forests, farmland and habitats; a dam failure is a disaster
Reliable, steady outputA drought cuts the output

Quick check

Which describes the overall energy change in a hydroelectric power station?

The water starts high and still and ends low; KE is only an in-between stage. D names that middle step, not the overall change.

GSolar power

Definition

At the top of Earth's atmosphere, a surface held perpendicular to sunlight receives about 1360 W m⁻². This near-constant irradiance is called the solar constant; it changes a little during the year.

  • Clouds and the atmosphere reflect or absorb a lot, so roughly half reaches the ground directly.
  • A surface tilted away from the rays catches less: multiply by \( \cos\theta \), where \( \theta \) is the angle of incidence (Lambert's cosine law, Section 1.3).
\[ P_\text{out} = \eta \times I A \cos\theta \]
  • Solar heating: a solar water heater has blackened tubes inside clear glass. They absorb the radiation, and hot water rises out by convection. It is simple, cheap and over 60% efficient.
  • Solar thermal electricity: curved mirrors focus sunlight on a pipe of oil. The hot oil boils water to drive a turbine, converting about 40–60% of the sunlight.
  • A photovoltaic (solar) cell is an n-type layer on a p-type layer, like an LED working backwards. The junction has an electric field from the n side to the p side.
  • Light gives an electron enough energy to leave its atom, leaving a hole. The field drives the electron to the n side and the hole to the p side.
  • In an external circuit, electrons flow from the n side round to the p side and fill the holes. The cell gives direct current; an anti-reflection coating on the glass lets more light in.

Worked example

A 1.6 m² solar panel has efficiency 18%. Sunlight of intensity 900 W m⁻² meets it at an angle of incidence of 35°. (a) Find its power output. (b) It works like this for 5 hours a day for 30 days. How much does it save if electricity costs $1.20 per kW h?

Show solution
  1. \( P = 0.18 \times 900 \times 1.6 \times \cos 35^\circ = 212\ \text{W} \).
  2. Energy \( = 0.212\ \text{kW} \times 5 \times 30 = 31.8\ \text{kW h} \).
  3. Saving \( = 31.8 \times 1.20 = \$38.2 \).

(a) About 210 W. (b) About $38.

No harmful by-products while runningDepends on the weather and the time of day

Quick check

True or false?

The solar constant is the solar power per unit area received at the Earth's surface.

It is measured at the top of the atmosphere, on a surface facing the Sun. Much less reaches the ground.

Photovoltaic cells are the only way to generate electricity from sunlight.

Solar thermal power stations focus sunlight to boil water and drive turbines.

Quick check

A panel gives power \( P_0 \) when light falls along its normal. The same light now meets it at an angle of incidence of 60°. The output becomes

\( \cos 60^\circ = 0.5 \). A uses \( \sin 60^\circ \), which treats 60° as the angle to the panel surface.
