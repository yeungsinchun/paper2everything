Goals

After this chapter you can:

  • compare appliances by end-use efficiency, and find the overall efficiency of a supply chain

  • read a grading-type energy label and use it to estimate running costs

  • explain luminous flux and luminous efficacy, and compare lamps by efficacy, lifetime and total cost

  • describe how filament, fluorescent and LED lamps produce light, using energy levels

  • find illuminance with the inverse-square law and Lambert's cosine law

2Definitions

End-use energy efficiency
Useful energy output as a percentage of the electrical energy input of an appliance
Luminous flux \( \Phi \)
Light given out per second, weighted by the eye's sensitivity; unit lumen (lm)
Luminous efficacy
Luminous flux per watt of electrical input; unit lm W⁻¹
Energy level
One of the allowed values of energy of an electron in an atom
Gas discharge
A current carried by an ionized gas
p-n junction
The boundary between n-type and p-type semiconductor in an LED or solar cell
Illuminance \( E \)
Luminous flux landing on each square metre of a surface; unit lux (lx = lm m⁻²)
Lambert's cosine law
A beam at angle \( \theta \) to the normal gives \( \cos\theta \) times the face-on illuminance

4Traps that cost marks

Three units, three quantities

Flux in lm, efficacy in lm W⁻¹, illuminance in lx. Swapping lm and lx is the commonest slip.

Angle from the normal

\( \theta \) in \( E = E_0\cos\theta \) is measured from the normal, not from the surface.

Square, not linear

Double the distance from a small lamp and \( E \) falls to a quarter, not a half.

When the law fails

No inverse-square law for a parallel beam or a long tube lamp close by; reflections make real readings higher.

Watts are not lumens

Equal light power in different colours does not look equally bright; green looks brightest.

Saving devices keep η

Timers and thermostats cut running time; they do not change an appliance's efficiency.
