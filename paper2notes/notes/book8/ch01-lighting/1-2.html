Lighting

ALuminous flux: brightness as the eye sees it

Definition

Luminous flux \( \Phi \) is the light given out by a source per second, weighted by how sensitive the human eye is to each wavelength. Its unit is the lumen (lm).

  • How bright a source looks depends on two things: the light power it gives out, and how the eye responds to its wavelengths.
  • One watt of light at 555 nm, where the eye is most sensitive, is defined as 683 lm. The same watt of red or violet light gives far fewer lumens.
Common mistake Dividing lumens by 683 gives watts only for pure 555 nm light. Real lamps give out many wavelengths, so the conversion does not hold.

Quick check

A red, a green and a blue lamp each give out 1 W of light. Which one looks brightest?

Equal light power does not mean equal brightness. The eye is most sensitive near 555 nm, so the green lamp gives the most lumens. "Equally bright" would be true only for equal luminous flux.

BLuminous efficacy: light per watt

Definition

The luminous efficacy of a lamp is its luminous flux divided by its electrical input power. Its unit is lm W⁻¹.

\[ \text{efficacy} = \frac{\Phi}{P_\text{in}} \]
  • For white lamps, higher efficacy means a bigger share of the input becomes light, so the lamp wastes less.
  • The theoretical maximum is 683 lm W⁻¹: every watt turned into 555 nm light.
  • In Hong Kong, energy labels on compact fluorescent lamps state the efficacy.
Exam tip Efficacy includes the eye's response; energy efficiency does not. Two lamps with the same efficiency can differ in efficacy if their colours differ.

Quick check

Which lamp looks brightest?

Worked example

A 15 W lamp has an efficacy of 60 lm W⁻¹. Find its luminous flux, and the energy it uses in a year if it is on 5 hours a day.

Show solution
  1. \( \Phi = 60 \times 15 = 900\ \text{lm} \).
  2. Energy \( = 0.015\ \text{kW} \times 5\ \text{h} \times 365 = 27.4\ \text{kW h} \).

900 lm; about 27 kW h a year.

CHow atoms give out light

Definition

An electron in an atom can only have certain values of energy, called energy levels. The atom is most stable with its electrons at the lowest levels.

\[ \begin{gathered} \Delta E = E_2 - E_1 = hf \\ h = 6.63 \times 10^{-34}\ \text{J s} \end{gathered} \]
  • Heating, collisions or absorbed radiation can lift an electron from \( E_1 \) to a higher level \( E_2 \). The excited state lasts only about \( 10^{-8} \) s.
  • When the electron drops back, the atom gives out an electromagnetic wave carrying the energy difference \( \Delta E \).
  • A larger \( \Delta E \) means a higher frequency \( f \). Visible light, about \( 4 \)–\( 7.5 \times 10^{14} \) Hz, needs a gap of the right size. Every lamp works by exciting atoms this way.

Quick check

True or false?

When an object gives out light, electrons are dropping from higher to lower energy levels.

The drop releases the energy difference as an electromagnetic wave.

A larger gap between two levels gives out light of lower frequency.

\( \Delta E = hf \): a larger gap gives a higher frequency, towards blue and ultraviolet.

DIncandescent (filament) lamp

  • A current heats a thin coiled tungsten filament to about 2000 °C or more. Its electrons jump between very many levels, so the light is a continuous spectrum.
  • Most of the output is invisible infrared, so efficacy is only about 10 lm W⁻¹ and a filament lamp gets hot.

A filament lamp is run at a higher temperature. Which of the following happen?

  1. A larger share of its output is visible light.
  2. Its efficacy rises.

EGas discharge: the fluorescent lamp

Definition

Gas discharge is a current carried by an ionized gas. A fluorescent tube lamp (FTL) is a sealed glass tube of low-pressure argon and mercury vapour, coated inside with phosphor.

  1. A large pd across the tube sets up an electric field. Electrons from a heated filament accelerate along the tube and ionize the argon, so a current flows.
  2. Electrons hit mercury atoms and excite them (without ionizing them). The mercury atoms drop back and give out mainly ultraviolet.
  3. The phosphor absorbs the UV and gives out visible light: fluorescence. Different phosphors give different colours, which mix to white.
PartJob
StarterGives the brief large pd that starts the discharge
BallastLimits and steadies the current once the gas conducts
Low-pressure argonIonizes easily, and gives electrons more chance to meet mercury atoms
  • A compact fluorescent lamp (CFL) is an FTL folded to fit a filament-lamp socket.
  • Runs cooler than a filament: about 50 lm W⁻¹ and 5000 to 24 000 hours. Most of the waste is heat in the discharge. The mercury makes disposal a concern.

Quick check

What is the mercury vapour in a fluorescent tube for?

Excited mercury atoms give out mainly UV; the phosphor turns that into visible light (not A). The filaments supply the electrons (not C), and the phosphor is excited by UV, not ionized (not D).

FLight-emitting diode (LED)

Definition

An LED is a p-n junction: n-type semiconductor (free electrons) joined to p-type semiconductor (holes, which act as positive carriers).

  • Forward connected (field from p to n inside): electrons and holes move to the junction. An electron falling into a hole drops to a lower level and gives out light.
  • The colour is set by the energy gap of the semiconductors, so one LED gives a narrow band of colours. White LEDs are usually blue LEDs with a coating that re-emits green to red.
  • An LED needs a low dc voltage: a lamp on the mains has a transformer to step the voltage down and a rectifier to turn ac into dc.
  • Efficacy up to about 100 lm W⁻¹; lifetime 25 000 to 100 000 hours. Heat in the semiconductor is the main loss and shortens its life.

Quick check

Two identical LEDs are connected in series to a cell. One of them is turned round. What happens?

The reversed LED blocks the current, and in a series circuit that stops the current through both. B would be the answer only if the LEDs were in parallel.

Quick check

True or false?

Every type of lamp makes light by heating something until it glows.

Only the filament lamp does. Fluorescent lamps use electron collisions and fluorescence; LEDs use electrons falling into holes.

Of the three types, only the filament lamp gives a continuous spectrum.

Fluorescent lamps give lines from their phosphors, and LEDs give narrow bands set by their energy gaps.
