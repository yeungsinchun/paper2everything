Section 1.3

Measuring illumination

How much light lands on a surface, and how that depends on the angle of the surface and its distance from the lamp.

What to remember

\( E = \Phi / A \)illuminance in lux (lx = lm m⁻²): light falling on each square metre

\( E = E_0 \cos\theta \)tilt a surface and the same beam spreads over more area

\( E = \dfrac{\Phi}{4\pi r^2} \)small source, surface facing it: double the distance, a quarter of the light

\( E = \dfrac{\Phi\cos\theta}{4\pi r^2} \)a surface on the floor below a lamp, with \( \cos\theta = d/r \)

AIlluminance: light on a surface

Definition

Illuminance \( E \) measures how much luminous flux lands on each square metre of a surface. Its unit is the lux (lx); \( 1\ \text{lx} = 1\ \text{lm m}^{-2} \). A lux meter measures it.

\[ E = \frac{\Phi}{A} \]
SituationTypical illuminance
Outdoors, bright sunshineabout 100 000 lx
Outdoors, heavy cloudabout 1000 lx
Office deskabout 500 lx
Street at night, under a lampabout 10 lx
Full moonlightabout 0.1 lx
Common mistake Three units, three quantities: luminous flux in lumens (lm), efficacy in lm W⁻¹, illuminance in lux (lx).

Quick check

Which is a unit of illuminance?

Illuminance is flux per area: lm m⁻², which is the lux. The lumen is for flux, lm W⁻¹ for efficacy, and W m⁻¹ is not a lighting unit.

BLambert's cosine law

Definition

Lambert's cosine law: when a parallel beam strikes a surface at an angle \( \theta \) to the normal, the illuminance is \( \cos\theta \) times its value at normal incidence.

\[ \begin{gathered} E = E_0 \cos\theta \\ A = \frac{A_0}{\cos\theta} \end{gathered} \]
  • \( E_0 \): illuminance with the surface facing the beam; \( \theta \): angle between the beam and the normal to the surface, not the surface itself.
  • The illuminance is greatest when the light strikes the surface normally (\( \theta = 0 \)).

Quick check

A surface facing a parallel beam has an illuminance of 500 lx. It is turned so the beam makes 60° with its normal. What is the illuminance now?

\( 500 \cos 60^\circ = 250 \) lx. 433 lx uses \( \sin 60^\circ \), measuring the angle from the surface instead of the normal. The flux reaching the surface is unchanged only if it stays face-on.

CThe inverse-square law

\[ E = \frac{\Phi}{4\pi r^2} \]
  • A small source spreads its flux \( \Phi \) evenly over a sphere of area \( 4\pi r^2 \), so \( E \propto 1/r^2 \).
  • It holds only for a small (point-like) source and a surface facing the light. It fails for a parallel beam, which does not spread, and for a long tube lamp close to the surface.

Quick check

A small surface 10 m from a small lamp has an illuminance of 1000 lx. It is moved to 30 m, still facing the lamp. What is the illuminance now?

Three times the distance gives \( 1/3^2 = 1/9 \): \( 1000/9 \approx 111 \) lx. 333 lx treats the fall as linear; 37 lx uses \( 1/3^3 \).

Worked example

A lux meter facing a small lamp reads 48 lx at 2.0 m. Find the lamp's luminous flux, and the reading at 6.0 m.

Show solution
  1. \( \Phi = 4\pi r^2 E = 4\pi (2.0)^2 (48) \approx 2410\ \text{lm} \).
  2. Three times the distance: \( E = 48 / 3^2 \approx 5.3\ \text{lx} \).

About 2400 lm; 5.3 lx at 6.0 m.

DA lamp above a floor: both laws together

\[ \begin{gathered} E = \frac{\Phi}{4\pi r^2}\cos\theta = \frac{\Phi}{4\pi d^2}\cos^3\theta \\ \cos\theta = \frac{d}{r} \end{gathered} \]
  • \( d \): height of the lamp above the floor; \( r \): distance from lamp to X; \( \theta \): angle between the ray and the normal at X.
  • The point directly below the lamp is brightest; the edge of a table or a room corner is the dimmest.
  • Real readings come out higher than these values, because walls and ceiling reflect extra light onto the surface.

Quick check

A small 3000 lm lamp is 3.0 m above the floor. Point X on the floor is 4.0 m (horizontally) from the point below the lamp. What is the illuminance at X?

\( r = 5.0 \) m and \( \cos\theta = 3/5 = 0.6 \), so \( E = 3000 \times 0.6 / (4\pi \times 5^2) \approx 5.7 \) lx. 9.5 lx forgets the cosine; 15.9 lx puts \( d^2 \) in place of \( r^2 \); 26.5 lx is the value directly below the lamp.

Worked example

A lamp hangs 1.5 m above the centre of a round table of radius 1.0 m. Every part of the table needs at least 30 lx. Find the smallest flux the lamp must give, and its power if its efficacy is 80 lm W⁻¹.

Show solution
  1. The edge is dimmest: \( r = \sqrt{1.5^2 + 1.0^2} = 1.80\ \text{m} \), \( \cos\theta = 1.5/1.80 = 0.832 \).
  2. \( \Phi = \dfrac{4\pi r^2 E}{\cos\theta} = \dfrac{4\pi (3.25)(30)}{0.832} \approx 1470\ \text{lm} \).
  3. Power \( = 1470 / 80 \approx 18\ \text{W} \). Reflections from walls would make this an overestimate.

About 1470 lm from an 18 W lamp.

PrecautionsMeasure \( r \) to the filament; cut reflections with dark surroundings; keep stray light off the meter.

Quick check

True or false?

Doubling the distance from a small lamp halves the illuminance.

It quarters it: \( E \propto 1/r^2 \).

Two small surfaces the same distance from a lamp always have the same illuminance.

Their angles to the light can differ, and \( E \) also depends on \( \cos\theta \).

The inverse-square law does not apply to a parallel beam of light.

A parallel beam does not spread out, so its illuminance does not fall with distance.
