ASpectra and temperature

Key idea

A hot dense object emits a continuous spectrum. Cooler gas in a star’s outer layers absorbs selected wavelengths, revealing dark lines.

O–B–A–F–G–K–M runs from hottest blue stars to coolest red stars. The Sun is G type.

Quick check

Which is hotter, O or M?

O stars have higher surface temperatures.

BLuminosity and temperature

Key idea

Luminosity is total emitted power. Stefan–Boltzmann gives L = 4πR²σT⁴; both radius and temperature matter.

Doubling radius multiplies luminosity by four. Doubling temperature multiplies it by sixteen at fixed radius.

Quick check

At fixed radius, doubling temperature changes luminosity by?

The fourth-power law gives a factor of sixteen.

Worked example: hotter star, same radius

Apply the fourth-power law

Compare two stars with equal radii. Star B has twice the surface temperature of star A.

  1. Use L ∝ R²T⁴. The radii cancel.
  2. LB/LA = (TB/TA)⁴ = 2⁴ = 16.

Star B emits 16 times the power.

CThe H–R diagram

Key idea

The H–R diagram places luminosity against temperature, which decreases from left to right. Main-sequence stars form a band; giants and white dwarfs occupy different regions.

A cool but luminous giant must have a large radius. White dwarfs are hot but dim.

Quick check

Why can a cool giant be very luminous?

Its large surface area can outweigh its lower temperature.
