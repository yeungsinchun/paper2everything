AEffective dose and biological effects

\[E=D\,w_R\,w_T\]

Absorbed dose measures energy deposited per mass. Radiation and tissue weighting factors account for differing biological effects. Effective dose is measured in sieverts (Sv); it is a risk-weighted quantity, not simply energy absorbed.

EffectDose relationship
Deterministichas a threshold; severity grows above it
Stochasticchance rises with dose; severity does not

Quick check

Which type of effect has a threshold dose?

Deterministic effects have a threshold. Above it, severity generally increases with dose.

BProtection principles

PrincipleMeaning
Justificationexpected benefit outweighs the radiation risk
Optimization (ALARA)keep exposure as low as reasonably achievable
Dose limitationkeep occupational and public exposure within limits
Time, distance, shieldingreduce time, increase distance, use suitable shielding

For a justified examination, optimize the procedure: use the shortest suitable exposure, keep unnecessary people away, and use shielding when appropriate. Clinical decisions balance diagnostic benefit and dose.

Quick check

Which set of actions reduces exposure?

Time, distance and shielding are practical ways to reduce exposure when they do not compromise the needed examination.

CComparing typical doses

Typical effective-dose values are approximate and vary with patient size and protocol. A dose comparison should support a clinical decision, not replace it. Avoid treating a single reference value as exact for every patient.

ExaminationApproximate effective dose
Chest radiograph20 μSv
Skull radiograph100 μSv
Abdominal radiograph700 μSv
Head CT2,000 μSv
Chest CT7,000 μSv
Abdominal CT8,000 μSv
Annual background (reference)2,400 μSv

These are broad reference values, not a prediction of an individual examination.

Quick check

Why are typical examination doses approximate?

Exposure and dose depend on the selected protocol, equipment and patient characteristics.
