AThe eye as an optical system

Definition

The cornea and the lens act together as one converging lens. They form a real, inverted, diminished image on the retina, the light-sensitive layer at the back of the eye.

  • Light passes the cornea, the aqueous humour, the pupil (the hole in the iris), the lens and the vitreous humour before it reaches the retina.
  • The cornea has a fixed shape. The ciliary muscle changes the shape of the lens, so the lens adjusts the focus.
  • The iris controls the pupil size: small in bright light, large in dim light.
  • The retina turns light into electrical signals. The optic nerve carries them to the brain, which also turns the image the right way up.
  • The eye detects only visible light, about 400 nm to 750 nm.

The blind spot is where the optic nerve leaves the eye. It has no rods or cones, so light landing there is not seen.

Quick check

Light enters the eye. At which surface is it refracted the most?

Bending depends on the change in refractive index. Air to cornea is 1.00 to 1.38, the largest step, so B. A and C are small steps (about 0.06): the lens only fine-tunes. D: the retina detects light; it does not focus it.

Quick check

(a) The image on the retina is upright and virtual.

False. A converging lens with the object far beyond its focal length gives a real, inverted, diminished image. The brain interprets it the right way up.

(b) The cornea changes shape to focus on near objects.

False. The cornea's shape is fixed. The lens changes shape, pulled or released by the ciliary muscle.

(c) In very dim light we see shades of grey because only the rods respond.

True. Rods are far more sensitive than cones but there is only one type, so they cannot tell colours apart.

BLens power and the eye as one lens

Definition

The power of a lens is \( P = 1/f \), with the focal length \( f \) in metres. Its unit is the dioptre (D), equal to m⁻¹. A converging (convex) lens has positive power; a diverging (concave) lens has negative power.

\[ P = \frac{1}{f} = \frac{1}{u} + \frac{1}{v} \]
\[ P_{\text{total}} = P_1 + P_2 \]
  • \( u \) is the object distance and \( v \) the image distance, both in metres. Real distances are positive; a virtual image has negative \( v \).
  • In the simplified eye, cornea and lens are one thin lens. The image distance \( v \) is the depth of the eyeball, about 2 cm, and it cannot change.
  • So to focus a nearer object (smaller \( u \), larger \( 1/u \)), the eye must increase \( P \).
  • Thin lenses close together add their powers. A cornea of about +40 D and a relaxed lens of about +20 D give an eye of about +60 D.

Quick check

A +6 D lens is placed against a −2 D lens. What is the focal length of the pair?

Powers add: \( +6 + (-2) = +4 \) D, so \( f = 1/4 = 0.25 \) m: C. A forgets that \( f \) is in metres. B adds the sizes, 6 + 2 = 8 D, ignoring the minus sign. D has the wrong sign: the pair still converges.

Worked example

Worked example

A simplified eye is 2.0 cm deep. Find its power when it looks at (a) a distant star and (b) a book 25 cm away.

  1. The image is always on the retina: \( v = 0.020 \) m.
  2. (a) Star: \( u \to \infty \), so \( 1/u = 0 \). \( P = 0 + 1/0.020 = 50 \) D.
  3. (b) Book: \( P = 1/0.25 + 1/0.020 = 4 + 50 = 54 \) D.

The eye adds 4 D of power to read at 25 cm.

CAccommodation, near point and far point

Definition

Accommodation is the eye changing the shape, and so the power, of its lens to focus objects at different distances. The lens-to-retina distance never changes.

ObjectCiliary muscleLensPower
far awayrelaxedpulled thinsmallest
nearcontractedbulges fatterlarger
  • The near point is the closest point the eye can focus. For a normal eye it is about 25 cm.
  • The far point is the farthest point the eye can focus. For a normal eye it is at infinity.
  • The power of accommodation is the extra power the eye can add: power at the near point minus power at the far point.
  • The power of accommodation falls with age as the lens stiffens, so the near point moves away.

Quick check

A student looks up from a phone to a mountain far away. What happens in the eye?

A far object needs less power, so the lens must be thinner. The ciliary muscle relaxes and the ligaments pull the lens thin: D. A is the near-object case. B mixes the two. C is how a camera focuses; the human eye changes shape, not distance.

Worked example

Worked example

An eye 2.0 cm deep has its near point at 40 cm and its far point at infinity. Find its power of accommodation.

  1. Far point: \( P_{\text{far}} = 0 + 1/0.020 = 50 \) D.
  2. Near point: \( P_{\text{near}} = 1/0.40 + 1/0.020 = 2.5 + 50 = 52.5 \) D.
  3. Power of accommodation \( = 52.5 - 50 = 2.5 \) D.

DResolving power

Definition

Two points are resolved when the eye sees them as separate. The resolving power is the eye's ability to do this: the smaller the angle it can separate, the better.

\[ \theta_{\min} \approx \frac{1.22\,\lambda}{D} \]
\[ \ell \approx r\,\theta \]
  • \( \theta_{\min} \) is in radians, \( \lambda \) is the wavelength and \( D \) the pupil diameter, in the same unit.
  • \( \ell \) is the distance between the two points and \( r \) their distance from the eye; this needs small angles in radians.
  • The pupil is a circular gap, so light diffracts through it and each point becomes a small blurred disc on the retina.
  • A larger pupil or a shorter wavelength gives a smaller \( \theta_{\min} \): better resolving power.
  • The spacing of the cones and imperfections of the lens also limit detail. \( \theta_{\min} \) is the best the eye could ever do.
Watch out

In dim light the pupil opens wider, so the diffraction limit is smaller. We still see less detail, because the lens edges blur the image and only the rods work.

Quick check

Which change lets an eye resolve two closer points?

\( \theta_{\min} \approx 1.22\lambda/D \): a larger \( D \) makes \( \theta_{\min} \) smaller, A. B makes \( \theta_{\min} \) larger. C shrinks the angle between the points, making it harder. D is backwards: a narrower gap diffracts more.

Write it

A screen is viewed from 0.50 m. Take the pupil as 3.0 mm and the light as 550 nm. Estimate the largest spacing between pixels that the eye cannot resolve.

\( \theta_{\min} = 1.22 \times 550 \times 10^{-9} / 3.0 \times 10^{-3} = 2.2 \times 10^{-4} \) rad.

\( \ell = r\theta_{\min} = 0.50 \times 2.2 \times 10^{-4} = 1.1 \times 10^{-4} \) m, about 0.1 mm. Pixels closer than this merge into a smooth picture.

Worked example

Worked example

A car's two headlamps are 1.4 m apart. At night the pupil is 5.0 mm wide; take the light as 600 nm. How far away can the car be before the lamps look like one?

  1. \( \theta_{\min} = 1.22 \times 600 \times 10^{-9} / 5.0 \times 10^{-3} = 1.46 \times 10^{-4} \) rad.
  2. \( r = \ell / \theta_{\min} = 1.4 / 1.46 \times 10^{-4} \).
  3. \( r \approx 9.6 \times 10^{3} \) m, about 10 km.

This is an upper limit. Real eyes merge the lamps sooner.

ESpectral response and colour

Definition

The spectral response of a receptor is how strongly it responds to light of each wavelength.

  • There is one type of rod and three types of cone. Each cone type covers a band of wavelengths; the bands overlap.
  • A colour is the ratio in which the three cone types are excited. Mixing three lights (red, green, blue) in the right ratio copies most colours; screens use this.
  • Taken together, the cones respond most near 555 nm, so green-yellow light looks brightest for the same intensity.
  • The rods peak near 505 nm. In dim light only rods work, so we see no colour.
  1. Light from an object is refracted by the cornea and the lens.
  2. An image forms on the retina.
  3. Rods and cones turn the light into electrical signals.
  4. The optic nerve carries the signals to the brain, which builds the picture and adjusts the lens if needed.

Quick check

Light of wavelength 600 nm enters the eye. Which cone types respond strongly?

At 600 nm the L curve is high, the M curve lower and the S curve near zero: B. That unequal mix is what we see as orange. C would look white. D is dim-light vision; at normal brightness the cones respond.

\[ \text{short sight: } f = -d_{\text{far}} \]

  • The concave lens takes rays from a very distant object and makes them seem to come from the eye's own far point \( d_{\text{far}} \): a virtual image there, so \( v = -d_{\text{far}} \).
  • The convex lens takes an object at 25 cm and forms a virtual image at the eye's own near point \( d_{\text{near}} \), so \( v = -d_{\text{near}} \).
  • A side effect of the concave lens: the near point moves a little farther away.
  • Bifocals: the upper part corrects far vision and the lower part is a convex reading lens.
  • Astigmatism (an unevenly curved cornea) brings lines in one direction into focus but not lines at right angles; a special non-spherical lens corrects it.

Quick check

A person can read comfortably but cannot read a bus number across the road. Which statement is correct?

Near is clear and far is blurred: short sight. The eye is too strong, so the far image forms in front of the retina, and a diverging lens fixes it: C. A and B describe long sight. D would spoil near vision, not far vision.

Quick check

A long-sighted person's near point is 75 cm. What lens power lets them read at 25 cm?

\( P = 1/0.25 + 1/(-0.75) = 4 - 1.33 = +2.7 \) D: A. B uses \( v = +0.75 \) m; the image is virtual, so \( v \) is negative. C has the sign of a short-sight lens. D is \( 1/0.75 \) alone.

Worked example

Worked example

A short-sighted eye has its far point at 2.5 m and its near point at 20 cm. (a) Find the power of the correcting lens. (b) Find the new near point with the lens on.

  1. (a) A distant object must give a virtual image at the far point: \( f = -2.5 \) m.
  2. \( P = 1/(-2.5) = -0.40 \) D, a concave lens.
  3. (b) The lens must put its image at the eye's near point: \( v = -0.20 \) m.
  4. \( 1/u = P - 1/v = -0.40 + 5.0 = 4.6 \) m⁻¹, so \( u = 0.217 \) m.
  5. The near point moves from 20 cm out to about 22 cm.
