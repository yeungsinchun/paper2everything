Quick check

Which part of the ear turns mechanical vibration into electrical signals?

The cochlea in the inner ear holds the hair cells that fire the nerve: C. A and B pass vibration on and amplify it but make no signals. D only guides sound to the eardrum.

BPressure amplification in the middle ear

\[ p_{\text{oval}} = \frac{1.3F}{A/17} \approx 22\,\frac{F}{A} = 22\,p_{\text{eardrum}} \]
  • The eardrum vibrates at the frequency of the sound. The three ear bones pass the vibration to the oval window.
  • The ear bones act as a lever and raise the force by about 1.3 times.
  • The eardrum is about 17 times larger in area than the oval window, so the same force gives a larger pressure.
  • The gain matters because the cochlea is full of liquid, which is much harder to set vibrating than air.

Quick check

In one ear the eardrum is 60 mm² and the oval window 3.0 mm², and the ear bones multiply the force by 1.3. By what factor does the pressure rise?

Area ratio \( 60/3.0 = 20 \); times the lever gain 1.3 gives 26: B. A divides by 1.3 instead of multiplying. C forgets the lever. D forgets the area ratio.

CThe cochlea sorts sound by frequency

Definition

The basilar membrane runs along the cochlea. It is narrow and stiff at the base (near the oval window) and wide and floppy at the apex, so each part resonates at a different frequency.

  • The cochlea is a coiled tube filled with liquid. Vibration enters at the oval window, travels up to the apex and back, and leaves at the round window.
  • High frequencies make the base vibrate most; low frequencies make the apex vibrate most.
  • Hair cells on the membrane fire the auditory nerve where it vibrates. The brain reads the place as the pitch.
  • Between about 60 Hz and 1000 Hz the ear can tell apart tones only 2 Hz to 3 Hz apart.

Quick check

A 15 kHz tone is played. Where does the basilar membrane vibrate most, and why?

A stiff, light strip has a high natural frequency, so high pitches resonate at the base: A. B swaps the ends. C would leave the ear no way to tell pitch. D is where vibration leaves, not where it is detected.

DIntensity and sound intensity level

Definition

The intensity \( I \) of a sound is the power passing through unit area at right angles to it, in W m⁻². From a point source it falls as \( 1/r^2 \).

Definition

The sound intensity level compares \( I \) with the threshold of hearing \( I_0 = 10^{-12} \) W m⁻² on a log scale. Its unit is the decibel (dB).

\[ L = 10\log_{10}\frac{I}{I_0} \]
\[ L_2 - L_1 = 10\log_{10}\frac{I_2}{I_1} \]
\[ I = \frac{P}{A} \]
  • The ear handles intensities from \( 10^{-12} \) to about \( 10^{2} \) W m⁻²: 14 powers of ten. A log scale squeezes this into 0 to 140 dB.
  • Doubling the intensity adds about 3 dB; halving takes off 3 dB.
  • ×10 adds 10 dB; ×100 adds 20 dB.
  • Long exposure above about 85 dB can damage hearing.
Watch out

Two sources together: add the intensities, then convert. Two 70 dB machines give 73 dB, not 140 dB.

Quick check

One hair dryer gives 76 dB at your ear. A second identical dryer is switched on beside it. What is the new level?

The intensity doubles, and \( 10\log 2 \approx 3 \) dB, so 79 dB: D. A adds decibels. B treats doubling as ×10. C ignores the second source.

Write it

A small loudspeaker gives 80 dB at 2.0 m. Treat it as a point source. Find the level at 8.0 m.

Four times the distance, so \( I \) falls to \( 1/4^2 = 1/16 \).

\( \Delta L = 10\log(1/16) = -12 \) dB, so the level is about 68 dB.

Worked example

Worked example

A sound has intensity \( 3.0 \times 10^{-6} \) W m⁻². (a) Find its level. (b) The source power is doubled. Find the new level.

  1. (a) \( L = 10\log(3.0 \times 10^{-6} / 10^{-12}) = 10\log(3.0 \times 10^{6}) \).
  2. \( L = 10 \times 6.48 = 64.8 \) dB.
  3. (b) Double power, double intensity: \( \Delta L = 10\log 2 = 3.0 \) dB.
  4. New level \( \approx 67.8 \) dB.

EHearing range, loudness and the phon

Definition

A curve of equal loudness joins sounds of different frequency that a listener judges equally loud.

Definition

The loudness of a sound in phons is the level in dB of a 1000 Hz tone that sounds equally loud. At 1000 Hz, phons and dB are the same number.

  • The threshold of hearing is the 0 phon curve: the quietest sound heard at each frequency. It is lowest near 3 kHz to 4 kHz, where the ear is most sensitive.
  • The curves rise at low and high frequencies: there the ear needs a higher level to hear the same loudness.
  • The threshold of discomfort is about 120 dB and the threshold of pain about 140 dB. The audible range lies between the threshold of hearing and these limits.
  • Equal loudness is not equal intensity. Loud curves are flatter than quiet ones.
  • Sound meters often use a dB(A) scale, weighted to follow the ear's response.

Quick check

(a) A 70 phon sound is as loud as a 70 dB tone at 1000 Hz.

True. That is the definition of the phon, with 1000 Hz as the reference.

(b) Two tones of 40 phon at 100 Hz and at 3 kHz have the same intensity.

False. They sound equally loud, but the ear is less sensitive at 100 Hz, so that tone needs a much higher level, and so a much higher intensity.

(c) The ear is most sensitive at 1000 Hz.

False. 1000 Hz is only the reference. The threshold curve is lowest near 3 kHz to 4 kHz.

Worked example

Worked example

From the curves, a 100 Hz tone must be at about 75 dB to sound as loud as a 60 dB tone at 1000 Hz. (a) What is its loudness? (b) How many times more intense is it than the 1000 Hz tone?

  1. (a) It matches a 60 dB tone at 1000 Hz, so its loudness is 60 phon.
  2. (b) Difference in level \( = 75 - 60 = 15 \) dB.
  3. \( I_2/I_1 = 10^{15/10} = 10^{1.5} \approx 32 \) times.
