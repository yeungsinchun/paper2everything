Section 24.3

Alternating current and r.m.s.

Reversing current and the d.c. equivalent that heats the same.

Learning objectives

Students should be able to:

  • distinguish d.c. and a.c.

  • define r.m.s., relate peak and r.m.s.

Ad.c. vs a.c.

Key idea

Direct current: direction constant (magnitude may be steady or pulsing). Alternating current: direction reverses periodically (usually sinusoidal \(I = I_0\sin\omega t\)). Mains is ~220 V r.m.s. at 50 Hz.

Key ideaDetail
Graphd.c. flat line; a.c. sine crossing zero.
PeriodT=1/f ≈20 ms at 50 Hz.

Concept check

Mains is 220 V r.m.s., 50 Hz sinusoidal. Peak voltage is about

V0=√2×Vrms≈311 V. Peak ≈1.414×rms.

Br.m.s. as heating equivalence

Key idea

r.m.s. (root mean square) of a.c. is the steady d.c. that dissipates heat at the same rate in a pure resistor. For sinusoidal: \(I_{\rm rms}=I_0/\sqrt{2}\), \(V_{\rm rms}=V_0/\sqrt{2}\). Power \(P = I_{\rm rms}V_{\rm rms}\) for resistive load; peak power is double average.

Key ideaDetail
Average powerSine: \(\bar P = I_0 V_0/2 = I_{\rm rms} V_{\rm rms}\).
Non-sineGeneral rms requires integration; not √2 except sine.

Concept check

Which controls heating of a resistor?

Heating ∝ Vrms^2/R. Peak matters only via rms. Frequency and direction irrelevant for resistive heating.
