Section 2.2

Heat capacity

How much energy one degree costs: for a whole body, and for each kilogram of a material.

Remember

Heat capacity\( C = E/\Delta T \), J °C⁻¹; depends on mass and material

Specific heat capacity\( c = E/(m\Delta T) \), J kg⁻¹ °C⁻¹; material only

Key equation\( E = mc\Delta T = C\Delta T \)

Heating rate\( \Delta T/t = P/(mc) \): heavier or higher \(c\) → slower

Goals

Students should be able to:

  • define heat capacity as \( C = \dfrac{Q}{\Delta T} \) and specific heat capacity as \( c = \dfrac{Q}{m\Delta T} \)

  • determine the specific heat capacity of a substance

  • solve problems involving heat capacity and specific heat capacity

AHeat capacity and specific heat capacity

Heat capacity

The heat capacity \(C\) of a body is the energy needed to raise its temperature by 1 °C. Unit: J °C⁻¹.

Specific heat capacity

The specific heat capacity \(c\) of a material is the energy needed to raise the temperature of 1 kg of it by 1 °C. Unit: J kg⁻¹ °C⁻¹.

\[ C = \frac{E}{\Delta T} \qquad c = \frac{E}{m\,\Delta T} \qquad E = mc\,\Delta T = C\,\Delta T \]

\(E\): energy absorbed or released (J) · \(m\): mass (kg) · \(\Delta T\): temperature change (°C) · \(C = mc\).

Common mistake

\(C\) belongs to a body and grows with its mass. \(c\) belongs to a material: 1 g and 1 tonne of copper have the same \(c\).

Quick check

The same amount of energy is given to each block below. Which one has the largest temperature rise?

D. \(\Delta T = E/(mc)\): the smallest \(mc\) wins. \(mc\) is 1800 (A), 4200 (B), 1560 (C) and 390 (D) J °C⁻¹. Comparing \(c\) alone or \(m\) alone is not enough.

Worked example Water and its kettle

A 1.5 kW kettle of heat capacity 300 J °C⁻¹ holds 0.80 kg of water at 20 °C. Ignoring losses, how long does it take to reach 100 °C?

  1. Both the water and the kettle warm by 80 °C. Total \(C = 0.80 \times 4200 + 300 = 3660\ \mathrm{J\,^\circ C^{-1}}\).
  2. \(E = C\Delta T = 3660 \times 80 = 2.93 \times 10^{5}\ \mathrm{J}\).
  3. \(t = E/P = 2.93 \times 10^{5} / 1500 = 195\ \mathrm{s}\).

about 195 s (3.3 min)

BMeasuring specific heat capacity

\[ c = \frac{E_2 - E_1}{m\,(T_2 - T_1)} \]

\(E_1, E_2\): joulemeter readings before and after · \(T_1\): starting temperature · \(T_2\): highest temperature reached.

PrecautionWhy
Polystyrene cup, lid, insulating mat; cotton wool round a metal blockcut heat loss, which would make the measured \(c\) too large
Heating element fully immersed (or fully inside the block)all the heater's energy goes into the sample
Stir the water; oil in the holes of a metal blockeven temperature; good thermal contact with the thermometer and the heater
Record the highest temperature, not the reading at switch-offthe hot element keeps giving heat for a while after switch-off

Quick check

In the electrical method, heat is lost to the surroundings. Compared with the true value, the measured specific heat capacity is

A. We divide all the supplied energy by \(m\Delta T\), but only part of it stayed in the water: \(c\) comes out too big. B gets the direction wrong. C: any loss matters, and it grows as the water gets hotter. D: loss makes the rise smaller, not larger.

Worked example From joulemeter readings

0.250 kg of water warms from 22.0 °C to 41.0 °C (highest reading) while the joulemeter goes from 15 600 J to 36 100 J. Find \(c\).

  1. \(E = 36\,100 - 15\,600 = 20\,500\ \mathrm{J}\); \(\Delta T = 41.0 - 22.0 = 19.0\ ^\circ\mathrm{C}\).
  2. \(c = E/(m\Delta T) = 20\,500/(0.250 \times 19.0) = 4320\ \mathrm{J\,kg^{-1}\,^\circ C^{-1}}\).
  3. It is above 4200 because some energy heated the cup and the air.

c ≈ 4.3 × 10³ J kg⁻¹ °C⁻¹

CHeating and cooling curves

\[ \text{slope of } T\text{–}t = \frac{\Delta T}{t} = \frac{P}{mc} \]
  • Same heater, steeper curve → smaller \(mc\). Same body, steeper curve → bigger \(P\).
  • Heat is lost faster the hotter the body is compared with the room, so a real heating curve bends and a cooling curve flattens out at room temperature.

Quick check

Two blocks of the same metal are heated by identical heaters. Block P's temperature rises three times as fast as block Q's. The ratio of mass P : Q is

C. Slope \(= P/(mc)\) with the same \(P\) and \(c\), so slope ∝ 1/m. Three times the slope means one third the mass. A inverts the relation.
