Goals

Students should be able to:

  • realise temperature as the degree of hotness of an object

  • explain the use of temperature-dependent properties in measuring temperature

  • define and use degree Celsius as a unit of temperature

ATemperature and thermometric properties

Definition

Temperature tells how hot or how cold a body is. In daily life we measure it in degrees Celsius (°C).

Thermometric property

A thermometric property is any property that changes steadily as a body gets hotter, so its value can stand for the temperature.

ThermometerProperty that changesGood for
Liquid-in-glasslength of a mercury or alcohol column (thermal expansion)everyday use, cheap, easy to read

Quick check

Three electrical parts are tested over the same temperature range. Part (1): its resistance stays the same. Part (2): its resistance rises steadily. Part (3): its resistance falls steadily. Which part(s) could be used to make a thermometer?

C. A property works if each temperature gives its own value. Rising or falling does not matter: a thermistor's resistance falls with temperature and it still makes a good thermometer. Part (1) is useless because every temperature gives the same reading.

BThe Celsius scale: two fixed points

Celsius scale

The ice point (0 °C) is the temperature of pure melting ice, and the steam point (100 °C) is the temperature of pure boiling water, both at normal atmospheric pressure. The gap between them is split into 100 equal divisions; each is one degree Celsius.

  • Both fixed points are good references: they stay constant and anyone can set them up again.
  • Impurities (salt in the ice, dissolved substances in the water) and a different air pressure shift both points, so the scale insists on pure water at 1 atm.
  • A narrow capillary and a large bulb make a sensitive thermometer: a small expansion of a lot of liquid shows as a long movement in a thin tube.
Exam tip

Sensitive means a large change in reading for a small change in temperature. It is not the same as accurate.

Quick check

Two liquid-in-glass thermometers are identical except that thermometer Q has a bigger bulb holding more liquid. Both columns are the same length in melting ice. When both are put in warm water,

B. More liquid expands by a larger volume for the same temperature rise, and the same narrow tube turns that into a longer movement. C mixes up how much liquid there is with how far it moves. D is wrong: once calibrated, both read the true temperature; Q just has longer degree marks.

CCalibration and reading by proportion

Calibration

Calibration means finding how a thermometric property varies with temperature (against a standard thermometer or the fixed points) and marking a scale from it.

If the property \(X\) varies linearly with temperature, two points fix the whole line, and similar triangles give any temperature:

\[ \frac{\theta - 0}{100 - 0} = \frac{X_\theta - X_0}{X_{100} - X_0} \quad\Rightarrow\quad \theta = \frac{X_\theta - X_0}{X_{100} - X_0}\times 100\ ^\circ\mathrm{C} \]

\(X_0\), \(X_{100}\): the property at the ice and steam points. \(X_\theta\): the property at the unknown temperature \(\theta\). \(X\) can be a length (cm), a resistance (Ω) or a current (mA).

  • Subtract the ice-point value first. The column is not zero length at 0 °C.
  • Draw a calibration graph when the property is not linear or when you have many readings: a best-fit line or curve beats two points.

Quick check

A thermometer with a linear scale was marked badly. It reads 6 °C in pure melting ice and 106 °C in pure boiling water. It now reads 46 °C. The true temperature is

A. The gap between the fixed points is still 100 scale divisions (106 − 6), so each division is 1 °C and only the zero is shifted: \(46 - 6 = 40\) °C. B ignores the offset. C divides by 106 instead of 100. D adds the offset instead of subtracting it.

Worked example Column length to temperature

A liquid column is 4.0 cm long at 5 °C and 22.0 cm long at 95 °C, and it expands linearly. (a) How long is it at 35 °C? (b) What is the temperature when it is 13.0 cm long?

  1. Length per degree: \( \dfrac{22.0 - 4.0}{95 - 5} = 0.20\ \mathrm{cm\ ^\circ C^{-1}} \).
  2. (a) From 5 °C to 35 °C is 30 °C, so the column grows by \(30 \times 0.20 = 6.0\) cm: length \(= 4.0 + 6.0 = 10.0\) cm.
  3. (b) From 4.0 cm to 13.0 cm is 9.0 cm, which is \(9.0 / 0.20 = 45\) °C above 5 °C.

(a) 10.0 cm  ·  (b) 50 °C
